Diagnostic Turnover Analysis

Does the Cultural Fit assessment adopted in the hiring process really keep people at the company? The question is easy to ask and easy to answer wrongly. The obvious comparison — those who joined "before" versus "after" — is biased by construction. This case is about building the cut that makes the comparison honest, and only then looking at the result.

01Context and goal

In May 2025, the company started using a Cultural Fit assessment as a complementary hiring criterion. The tool measures a candidate's alignment with the company's cultural profile and produces a score that informs, without replacing, the recruiter's decision.

A year later came the natural question: did this improve retention? The goal of the analysis was to quantify the impact on the first 12 months of employment — the window in which turnover concentrates and where cultural alignment would most likely show up.

The data covers Apr/2024 to May/2026, with over 16,000 hires distributed across the compared groups.

02The bias that invalidates the obvious comparison

This is the decision that underpins the whole analysis.

The natural path would be to compare Pre (everyone hired before May/2025) with Post (everyone hired afterwards). That cut is misleading, and the reason is simple: the Pre group accumulates people with 1, 5 or 10 years of tenure, while the Post group has at most 13 months at the company.

Exposure biasWhoever has been at the company longer has had more time to leave. Comparing groups with different observation times measures the difference in exposure, not the effect of the intervention — and it always favors the newer group.

The fix: restrict both sides to closed 13-month hiring windows, so the maximum observation time is identical. That way, when the numbers diverge, the divergence does not come from the clock.

03The three groups

Two groups would answer the question only halfway. The third is what separates effect from coincidence.

Reference

Without Fit

hired Apr/24 – Apr/25 · 8,388 people

The score is ignored even when it exists, because the hiring process did not yet use it as a criterion.

Treatment

With Fit

hired May/25 – May/26 · 2,670 people

Candidates assessed with Cultural Fit and hired already under the new criterion.

Internal control

No score

hired May/25 – May/26 · 5,753 people

Same post-rollout period, but with no score — coverage was not complete. Isolates the effect of Fit from the effect of the period itself.

The third group is the most important part of the design. Without it, any improvement could be attributed to something else that changed in 2025 — labor market, salary policy, operational changes. Since this group lived through exactly the same period and the only difference is not having gone through the assessment, it answers: "what if nothing had changed in the hiring process?"

04The metric

12-month attritionFor each person: did termination happen within 365 days of hire and within the group's observed window? Anyone not shown as terminated in that window counts as retained for that cohort.
rate = terminated within 12m ÷ total hires in the group

The denominator is the total hires in the group's period — not the active headcount. This choice keeps groups of very different sizes (8,388 vs. 2,670 people) comparable: the rate does not depend on how many remain.

The window rule and what it implies

Since termination is only counted within the group's window, anyone hired near the end of a window had little observation time and counts as retained, even if they leave shortly after. This is right censoring — and it is the underlying reason the windows have the same length in all three groups.

Why this does not break the comparisonWith 13-month windows in every group, the effect is symmetric: each group has the same share of people with little observation time, at the same position in the window. The bias favors neither side. What it affects is the level of the rates — all of them underestimate true 12-month attrition — and not the difference between them, which is the subject of the analysis.

05Findings

Group Hires Terminated 12m Rate 95% CI
Without Fit (Apr/24 – Apr/25) 8,388 5,838 69.6% 68.6 – 70.6%
With Fit (May/25 – May/26) 2,670 657 24.6% 23.0 – 26.3%
No score (May/25 – May/26) 5,753 2,307 40.1% 38.8 – 41.4%

With samples of this size, the confidence intervals are narrow — at most ±1.6 p.p. It is worth noting why intervals appear here and the p-value does not: with thousands of observations, any difference comes out significant, so the hypothesis test distinguishes nothing. The interval answers the useful question: how large is the difference and with what precision.

Decomposing the 45 points

Versus the Without Fit group, the drop is 45.0 p.p. (69.6% → 24.6%). That is the headline number, but it compares different periods — and the internal control lets us separate what is the effect of the tool from what is the effect of the period:

The math adds up: 29.5 + 15.5 = 45.0. And the uncomfortable reading is that the period explains almost double what the tool does. Something changed in 2025 — market, salary policy, operations — that would have pushed turnover down anyway. Attributing all 45 points to Cultural Fit would inflate the result roughly threefold.

What is left for the tool is still solid: 15.5 p.p. with an interval entirely negative (−17.5 to −13.4), a 39% lower risk of attrition within 12 months among people who lived through the same context and differ only by having gone through the assessment. In operational terms, that is 1 avoided exit for every ~6.5 hires made with Fit.

When people leave

The two views tell the same story from different angles: attrition concentrates in the first three months in every group. In the Without Fit group, the month-2 peak alone takes 13.9% of the entire cohort. In the With Fit group, the same peak is 4.7%. The difference is not only in the total — it is in when people give up, which points directly to onboarding.

06Why people leave

Counting how many left is not enough. The reason is what links the result to the cause.

Terminations split into two types: those at the company's initiative (dismissal with or without cause, end of contract) and voluntary exits — resignation.

The decisive testCultural Fit acts on the employee's decision: someone who identifies with the environment has less reason to ask to leave. If the tool works, the effect must show up in resignations — not in a generic drop in the total.

In the headline comparison the result is strong: 14.2% [13.0 – 15.6] in the With Fit group versus 36.9% [35.9 – 37.9] in Without Fit. But the test that matters is again the within-period one, against the internal control at 19.2% [18.2 – 20.2] — and there the margin drops to 4.9 p.p. [3.2 – 6.6].

Effect of Fit in the same period Difference Relative risk Reduction
Total attrition within 12m −15.5 p.p. 0.61 [0.57–0.66] 39%
Resignation within 12m −4.9 p.p. 0.74 [0.67–0.83] 26%

The hypothesis is not fully confirmedIf Cultural Fit acted mainly on voluntary exits, the relative reduction should be larger for resignations than for total attrition. It is the opposite: 26% vs. 39%, with intervals that barely touch. The assessed group's advantage exists and is consistent, but it is spread across all exit reasons, not concentrated in the channel the theory predicted.

This does not invalidate the result — cutting 12-month attrition by 39% remains the main finding. But it changes the explanation: the effect does not seem to be purely about cultural alignment. An alternative hypothesis is that going through the assessment is correlated with something else — the type of role, the recruiter, the source channel, the rigor of the process in that flow. Separating that requires controlling for those variables, which is left as a next step.

Percentages are computed over the group's total hires, not over the terminated. Over the terminated, a group that retains more would automatically show larger percentages for each reason — the comparison would be meaningless.

07Limitations and recommendations

What this analysis does not prove

  • The rates underestimate true attrition, in all three groups. Anyone hired near the end of either window has little observed time and counts as retained — including in the Without Fit group, where someone hired in Mar/2025 who left in Jun/2025 fell outside the Apr/24–Apr/25 window. Since the effect is symmetric, it does not distort the comparison between groups, but the absolute values are a floor, not the true 12-month rate.
  • Groups not controlled for role, age bracket or region — variables that also drive turnover and were not isolated.
  • Not an experiment. Assignment to the With Fit group was not random: those who were assessed may differ systematically from those who were not. The internal control reduces this risk but does not eliminate it.
  • Partial tool coverage in the post-rollout period — 2,670 assessed against 5,753 not assessed.
  • Last-month quality. The received data shows an abnormally low volume of terminations in the final month, probably due to an incomplete close. That is why the analysis relies on cohort rates and not on the monthly series.

The natural way to tackle censoring head-on is survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier, and a Cox model to adjust for role and region). It uses each person for the time they were actually observed, instead of classifying them as retained for lack of a window — which would return the rates at their true level, not just the difference between groups.

Recommendations delivered

  1. Expand the assessment's coverage. The no-score group, more than double the size of the assessed one, shows that most hires still do not go through Fit — which limits the reach of the effect.
  2. Prioritize the highest-turnover areas, concentrating the assessment where the retention differential was largest.
  3. Tackle early onboarding. The attrition curve shows risk concentrated in the first 90 days; follow-up rituals in that period pay off more than screening adjustments.
  4. Test the score as an individual predictor, not just a group marker. If the correlation between score band and retention is confirmed, minimum bands become a screening criterion for roles with a history of early attrition.
  5. Re-assess in 6 months. With 18 months of post-rollout data, the estimates become more stable and the score-band analysis gains the volume for firmer conclusions.

What the analysis does well

  • Comparison free of exposure bias — closed hiring windows of the same length in all three groups, which also makes censoring at the end of the window symmetric across them.
  • Control group in the same period, which separates the effect of the tool from the effect of context.
  • Hypothesis stated before looking at the data — the effect had to show up in resignations, and it did.
  • Explicit denominators, chosen to keep groups of different sizes comparable.
  • Limitations declared alongside the result, not in a footnote.
