Candidate Matching by Embedding

Rank the candidates in a talent pool by content relevance to a new job opening, starting from the résumé PDF. The interesting work was not generating the embedding — it was finding where it fails on its own (years of experience, negation) and replacing each blind spot with a deterministic rule, without losing semantic coverage everywhere else.

01The problem

When a client migrates onto the platform, they commonly bring a large base of candidates already on file — a talent pool of thousands of résumés. When that client opens a new job, there is no practical way to quickly find a group of relevant candidates in that base: the search depends on manual filters and reading résumés one by one. I prototyped a pipeline that extracts text from the PDF, generates an embedding for both résumé and job description, and ranks by similarity — with an explanation of why, not just a number.

What the score measures (and doesn't)Content relevance — how much the résumé text talks about the same things the job asks for. It is not a quality score for the candidate, nor does it measure potential, cultural or behavioral fit: in the test dataset those signals already exist as separate columns (Reasoning, Social, Motivational, Cultural fit).

02From job opening to vector

Extraction → sections → embedding → cosine similarity, all stored in DuckDB.

Each résumé's text is extracted from the PDF (pdfplumber) and split into 5 sections — header, summary, experience, education and skills. Each section becomes its own vector, plus an "overall" vector for the whole document: 5 embeddings per résumé. The model is google/embeddinggemma-300m, from the Gemma 3 line — 300 million parameters, runs on CPU, no GPU required, producing 768-dimensional vectors. The job description uses the model's query prompt and the résumé the document prompt — EmbeddingGemma was trained specifically for these asymmetric retrieval roles.

A script (build_store.py) hashes each résumé's text and only re-embeds what changed or is new — adding a résumé does not reprocess the whole base. Vectors live in a local DuckDB file (data/vetores.duckdb), which supports native arrays with array_cosine_similarity: ranking runs directly in SQL, with no separate database server.

047 out of 10 right candidates at the top

Sanity test with known answers ahead of time, across 8 openings — in data science this is called "precision@10."

Every résumé in the test dataset (82 in total) was created with a target job opening already in mind — meaning I knew the correct answer beforehand. To check whether the system works, I ran 8 real job descriptions against the whole base and counted: out of the top 10 results for each search, how many actually belonged to the right opening? On average across the 8 openings, the system got 7 out of 10 right (68%) — worst case, 6 out of 10; best case (HR Director), 9 out of 10.

What this test shows (and doesn't)It measures whether the system brings the right people close to the top — not whether the exact order among them is perfect. It's a deliberately simple test, meant to catch regressions when I change the model or pipeline later, not a final quality score for the product.

05Two blind spots fixed by rule

The embedding captures topic very well and numbers almost not at all. Rather than forcing the model to learn arithmetic, I handled it outside the model.

Years of experience: from similarity to arithmetic

I tested a "5+ years of experience" requirement against résumés describing 2, 5, 6, 7, 10 and 15 years. The embedding alone barely told low and high experience apart.

Before · embedding only

Score against "5+ years of experience"

2 years → 0.39 5 years → 0.41 17 years → 0.46

The gap between 2 and 17 years is nearly invisible to the model — it picks up the topic "years of experience," not the number itself.

After · rule + embedding

Count extracted and compared numerically

meets/exceeds → max score 3 of 5 years → 0.60 no clear date → no penalty

A regex extracts the minimum required from the opening; experience dates ("Feb/2021 – present") and spelled-out numbers ("six years of experience") sum the candidate's years, using the larger of the two sources.

This item shows up in the requirement checklist tagged as type quantitative. When a candidate's years can't be extracted, the item simply drops out of the average — it doesn't penalize for missing information, it just stops contributing that one signal.

Technical terms: an acronym isn't a synonym to the embedding

Why mix a rule with the embeddingA small, conservative dictionary of known technical terms (Python, SQL, Power BI, AWS, Docker, BI...) with their synonyms and acronyms checks for a literal match in any résumé section, and the result is blended 50/50 with the semantic score. This fixes two failure modes of the embedding alone: finding a "similar-looking" section for a tool that isn't actually there, and not recognizing an acronym well (BI vs. Business Intelligence). Outside the dictionary, it stays pure embedding — on purpose, so it doesn't turn into an endless list of rules.

06Section scores + requirement checklist

Two layers of explanation, neither of them a bare number.

Beyond the overall score, the job description is compared section by section of the résumé (summary, experience, education, skills) — showing which part weighed most in the result. And the opening is broken into requirements (bullets under "required" / "nice-to-have"), each compared against the résumé's best-matching section — turning into a requirement-by-requirement checklist, not a single opaque score.

Real example: "Mid-Level Data Analyst" opening

The top-ranked candidate (overall score 0.555) had "Senior Developer" as their original target opening in the dataset — not "Data Analyst." That's the point: the system compares content, not a fixed category, and a Data Engineer in career transition can be more relevant to the opening's text than someone labeled under the "right" category.

The checklist uses embeddings, not literal keyword search — so it picks up synonyms ("advanced Excel" matches "proficient with spreadsheet tools"), but it can also "find" a similar-looking section without the requirement actually being met. Treat it as a relative ranking, not a binary met/not-met stamp.

07Why DuckDB, not Postgres

For a local prototype, DuckDB avoids installing and maintaining a separate database server. It already supports native arrays with array_cosine_similarity, so ranking runs directly in SQL inside the database itself — the same logic as a pgvector query in production.

An isolated, reversible decisionIf the project grows (multiple users, production), the swap is contained in src/store.py — to Postgres+pgvector, without touching the rest of the pipeline (extraction, sections and embeddings stay the same).

08The app and how to run it

Streamlit on top of the same modules used by the test notebooks — a single source of truth for the backend.

python src/extract_curriculos.py   # reads the PDFs, extracts text
python src/build_store.py          # generates embeddings, writes to DuckDB
streamlit run app.py               # opens the app
  • The threshold never hides a candidate. Anyone below the checklist's minimum score doesn't disappear — they go into a collapsed expander ("candidates hidden by the threshold"), still visible on request.
  • Candidate registration without a script. A dedicated tab accepts the PDF (or pasted text directly), extracts it, splits it into sections, generates the embeddings and makes the candidate searchable within the same session.
  • Two companion notebooks. playground_embeddings.ipynb tests loose embeddings on any pair of phrases; teste_matching_vagas.ipynb audits the whole pipeline line by line, using the same modules as the app.

09Limitations

Negation is poorly capturedI tested "I have X" against "I don't have X" for the same opening: the score difference was only 0.03 to 0.08 (on a 0.4–0.7 scale) — while a résumé that simply never mentions the topic scores below 0.15–0.47. In other words, explicitly denying a requirement still scores better than not mentioning it at all. This is a known limitation of dense embeddings in general, not a bug in this pipeline: the model captures topic far better than logical polarity (affirmation vs. negation). The bigger risk sits on the job description side — a phrase like "no prior experience required" turning into a checklist item can be treated as the opposite of what the opening actually asks for. Not fixed yet: for now, the job description needs a manual review before pasting it in.

  • Scanned PDFs don't extract. pdfplumber can't read a PDF without a selectable text layer; the app warns and lets you paste the text manually instead.
  • The semantic checklist can "find" a false match. Without the technical-term dictionary, the embedding can pull a résumé section close to a requirement without it actually being met — treat it as indicative, not a binary stamp.
  • One résumé is missing from the original test base. One of the 83 candidates in the spreadsheet never had its PDF file — that candidate never appears in any result. Documented, not fixed.
  • DuckDB allows one writer at a time. On Windows, opening the app and a notebook at the same time against the same file blocks one process until the other releases the connection.
  • The score doesn't evaluate the candidate. It only measures content relevance — potential, cultural and behavioral fit remain separate signals, outside this pipeline's scope.
